Forum-discovery note: Public candidate discussions consistently describe governance scenarios as multi-subscription enterprises that must enforce tagging, structure management groups for regulatory or data-residency requirements, and choose between Azure Policy, RBAC, and resource locks for a stated outcome. Recurring themes include tag inheritance behavior, the management-group depth limit, and when a new subscription is warranted versus a new resource group. Coverage notes:

- This task is fragmented across product areas rather than served by one documentation set. Architecture guidance is more useful here than any single product root: the Cloud Adoption Framework carries the design reasoning, while the Azure management groups, Azure Resource Manager, and Azure Policy sets carry the enforceable limits and mechanics.
- Download the Cloud Adoption Framework first — five of the thirteen links live in it and it is the only source that gives you a defensible reference hierarchy. Follow with Azure Resource Manager (tags, tag policies, resource groups), Azure management groups, and Azure Policy. Add Microsoft Cost Management if cost allocation or chargeback appears in the scenario.
- Memorize the numeric limits; they turn design questions into arithmetic. Six levels of management group depth excluding root and subscription, 10,000 management groups per directory, one parent per management group or subscription, 50 tag name-value pairs per resource/resource group/subscription, 512-character tag names and 256-character tag values, and 800 instances of a resource type per resource group.
- The single most testable tagging fact is that **tags are not inherited**. Resources do not receive tags from their resource group or subscription, and management groups cannot be tagged at all. Any scenario requiring consistent tags on existing resources needs Azure Policy `modify` plus a remediation task; `append` only affects create and update operations. Cost Management tag inheritance is a separate, billing-only mechanism that stamps usage records, not the resources themselves.
- Distinguish the three control types that scenarios deliberately confuse. Azure Policy governs resource *state* regardless of who acts; Azure RBAC governs *who* may act; resource locks (CanNotDelete, ReadOnly) override all users and roles for control-plane operations and cannot be applied to management groups. Locks and RBAC are adjacent to this task rather than central, but they are frequent distractors.
- Watch for the anti-patterns CAF explicitly names: do not duplicate the org chart in a deep hierarchy, do not create management groups for dev/test/prod (use separate subscriptions in the same management group), do not create management groups per region unless data residency or sovereignty requires it, and do not assign application-team RBAC at management group scope.
- Recognize the billing hierarchy as separate from the resource hierarchy. EA uses billing account → department → enrollment account, MCA uses billing account → billing profile → invoice section, and neither maps onto management groups. Management-group cost rollups exclude reservations and Marketplace purchases and are unsupported for MCA.
- Lightly covered but plausibly testable: CAF naming conventions and resource abbreviations (name permanence versus tag mutability is the design tradeoff — put only immutable facts in names), subscription vending as the automated provisioning path for subscription democratization, subscription-to-tenant transfer restrictions, and Azure Blueprints retirement in favor of deployment stacks and Terraform/Bicep-based landing zone modules.
